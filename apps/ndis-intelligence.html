NDIS Intelligence

The register, read.

Five saved queries over the NDIS Commission's own compliance register and providers' own ACNC filings. Every figure is a published number or arithmetic over published numbers. Nothing here is an allegation about any provider — each tab says what its numbers can and cannot support.

The register as a time series

Enforcement that appeared between two extracts

The Commission publishes dated extracts showing current state only, so nothing published shows when an action arrived. This diffs two extracts.

An appearance is dated to the interval between two extracts, not to a day.The register's own effective date often precedes publication by months, so a row appearing now may concern conduct years old.

Run to diff the two most recent extracts.
Batches, not a stream

Compliance notices by the day they took effect

Notices arrive in mass batches on single dates, and overwhelmingly cite one instrument — the section 73J audit obligation.

Because notices arrive in batches, a provider's presence in this register is weak evidence about that provider — it may mean only that it sat inside a mass audit-obligation batch.Read the action type and its narrative, never the mere presence of a hit.

Run to count notices per effective date.
What the register does not say

How specifically each action type explains itself

How many rows of each type state a basis — "on the basis that…", "reasonably believes", "convicted of" — versus only describing what is prohibited or required.

A low percentage is a fact about the register's publishing practice, not about the people named in it.Banning-order narratives state the scope of the prohibition rather than the conduct behind it; that is why the figure for banning orders is low.

Run to measure the register's own explanatory detail.
Two years of a provider's own filings

Providers whose labour costs collapsed as a share of revenue

Employee expenses falling sharply as a share of service revenue while revenue holds or grows: the work is now done by someone other than employees.

This is an anomaly in a self-report, not an allegation. Moving to labour hire or contracting is lawful and common, and a reporting-period change or restructure produces the same shape.Checked independently: none of these providers appears in any tribunal or court document under its exact name. The shape is unexplained, which is not evidence of sham contracting.

Run to compare FY23 and FY24 filings.
Where the money goes

Providers whose money leaves as unspecified "other expenses"

Most of the money leaving as neither wages nor named grants — the brokerage and plan-management shape, and the one place a public dollar's final recipient is named in no register.

Lawful and common: brokering or managing others' services pays most funding out by design.The AIS reports one government-revenue line covering everything a charity does, so a provider's figure may be aged care or housing rather than NDIS money.

Run to see the pass-through cohort.
